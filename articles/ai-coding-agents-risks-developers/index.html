When the Agent Writes the Code, Are You Still the Developer?

Coding agents can accelerate delivery while creating knowledge debt, weaker skill formation, and shallow review. The real risk is losing ownership of what ships.

By Islam Darawsheh September 7, 2026 16 min read

You ask an AI coding agent to implement what sounds like a small feature. You leave it working for a few minutes and return to find that it has modified twenty files, added a database migration, changed several service registrations, and written a new test suite. It then reports, confidently, that the task is complete and every test has passed.

The result looks impressive. But there is a more important question than the number of changed files or the reassuring line of green checks:

Do you actually know what happened inside your project?

Can you explain the decisions the agent made? Do you know which assumptions shaped its solution? Did you verify that the tests validate the intended behavior, or did you merely notice that they passed? If the feature fails in production next week, will you know where to begin?

These questions are not an argument against AI coding agents. Modern agents can save real time and complete work that once took developers hours. However, the transition from an assistant that suggests a few lines of code to an agent that explores a repository, executes commands, edits files, and makes a chain of decisions has changed the nature of the risk.

The problem is no longer limited to AI suggesting an incorrect line of code. The new danger is that it can complete an entire sequence of work while the developer gradually loses both an understanding of what happened and genuine ownership of the decisions made.

An Agent Is Not Just Smarter Autocomplete

Traditional code completion suggested a function or a few lines while the developer remained inside the implementation process. A modern coding agent can:

  • Explore the repository and identify files related to the task.
  • Create a plan and execute it across multiple steps.
  • Modify dozens of files in a single session.
  • Run builds, tests, linters, and static-analysis tools.
  • Add dependencies, migrations, and configuration.
  • Detect some of its own failures and attempt new solutions.
  • Declare the task complete without requiring the developer to inspect every step.

That capability is the source of an agent’s value. It is also the source of its risk. The more independently it can operate, the wider the possible gap between what happened in the codebase and what the developer understands about it.

The Junior Developer’s Risk: Results Without a Mental Model

Junior developers have traditionally learned through a repetitive cycle: write something, make a mistake, read the error, investigate it, try another approach, and eventually understand why one solution worked while another failed. That cycle is slow and sometimes painful, but it builds a mental model of the system.

An agent can produce a working application without requiring the developer to experience much of that cycle. After a while, the developer may appear capable of delivering complex features even though their ability to read, diagnose, and reason about the system has not developed at the same rate.

They can demonstrate the result but may struggle to answer questions such as:

  • Why was this design selected instead of a simpler alternative?
  • Which dependency-injection lifetime is appropriate here, and why?
  • What happens if two requests reach this code simultaneously?
  • Where does the transaction begin and end?
  • Does this test validate real behavior, or only prove that a mock returned the configured value?
  • Does the solution fit the surrounding architecture, or does it merely work in isolation?

This creates an illusion of competence: the developer’s ability to produce professional-looking output rises much faster than their ability to understand it and make independent engineering decisions.

A 2026 Experiment: Faster Completion Did Not Mean Better Understanding

In January 2026, Anthropic published a randomized controlled trial involving 52 software engineers, most of them junior developers. Participants learned a Python library, completed programming tasks with it, and then took a test covering conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging.

The AI-assisted group completed the practical task about two minutes faster on average, but the speed difference was not statistically significant. The difference in understanding was significant: the AI group scored an average of 50% on the assessment, compared with 67% for the group that coded without AI. The largest gap appeared in debugging questions—the exact skill developers need when generated code is wrong.

Importantly, AI use did not produce the same outcome for everyone. Participants who delegated the implementation and debugging directly to AI were among the fastest, but they showed some of the weakest understanding. Those who asked conceptual questions, requested explanations, or checked their understanding after generation retained substantially more knowledge.

There is an important limitation: the experiment used an AI assistant inside a coding environment, not a fully autonomous product such as Claude Code or Codex. It therefore does not prove that every coding agent reduces learning. It does, however, provide recent evidence that the method of delegation can deliver faster output at the expense of the skills required to evaluate and maintain that output. When Errors Disappear, Some Learning Disappears With Them

We usually treat errors as wasted time. Yet much of a developer’s expertise is formed while discovering why something failed, not while typing the final correct implementation.

A developer who personally investigates a race condition becomes more alert to concurrency risks in the future. Someone who discovers that a stateful service was registered as a singleton rarely forgets the importance of service lifetimes. A developer who ships a bug despite having a green test suite learns the difference between increasing coverage and creating a meaningful safety net.

If the agent encounters and resolves all those problems away from the developer’s attention, the product may improve while the knowledge that should have formed during its construction is lost.

This does not mean forcing developers to reinvent every solution or banning them from using AI. It means refusing to confuse task completion with skill acquisition. They are different outcomes, and one can improve while the other deteriorates.

Experienced Developers Face a Different Risk

Experienced developers do not usually lack the fundamentals. Their problem begins when their expertise becomes a reason not to follow the work closely:

I know the architecture. If the agent makes a serious mistake, I will catch it later.

At first, they delegate repetitive work. Then they delegate an entire feature. Eventually, they allow several agents to process multiple tasks in parallel. As the volume of generated work increases, meaningful review becomes harder. They read the summary, check whether the tests passed, scan selected parts of the diff, and approve the result.

The experienced developer does not suddenly lose years of knowledge. Their relationship with the project changes gradually: they move from being the engineer who constructs and understands decisions to the person managing a stream of output produced faster than they can absorb it.

Calling this simply “laziness” would be emotionally satisfying but technically incomplete. It is usually a combination of three effects:

  1. Cognitive offloading: A growing share of reasoning and verification is delegated to the tool.
  2. Review fatigue: The developer must continuously review changes that are too large or too numerous for careful inspection.
  3. Automation habituation: Because the tool’s output is acceptable most of the time, scrutiny gradually becomes less rigorous.

A 2026 Study Found More Approvals and Less Scrutiny

A study published in June 2026 analyzed 11,429 reviews performed by 400 people who repeatedly reviewed pull requests created by coding agents over seven months.

Approval increased between reviewers’ early and later interactions. When each reviewer’s experience was divided into ten stages, approval rates rose from 27.9% to 42.4%, while change-request rates fell from 11.2% to 5.6%. At the same time, the number of inline review comments declined by 22%, and the total number of words in those comments fell by 28%.

This pattern is consistent with reviewers becoming habituated to agent output and inspecting it less closely. It does not prove that laziness caused the change; agent quality might also have improved during the observation period. The more defensible conclusion is that the data showed an increase in approvals accompanied by a measurable reduction in visible review effort. Habituation at the Gate

That distinction matters because assurances about safe agentic development often end with the phrase “a human will remain in the loop.” But placing a human name on the approval does not prove that the person understood the change. The value of review comes from its depth, not merely from its existence.

A July 2026 enterprise case study shows how quickly this can become a capacity problem. Across 802 developers and 196,212 pull requests, per-developer throughput eventually reached 2.09 times the pre-mandate baseline. Yet the review load per reviewer also roughly doubled, and automated review overtook human review. The study covered one AI-forward company and adoption was not randomly assigned, so it should not be treated as a universal productivity forecast. It does demonstrate the operational tension clearly: generation can scale faster than careful human inspection. AI Writes Faster Than Humans Can Review

Does the Agent Understand What We Actually Asked For?

An agent can write technically valid code and still complete the wrong task. It may exceed the requested scope, apply what it considers a best practice despite an explicit project decision, or announce success before verifying a critical requirement.

A large study published in May 2026 examined 20,574 real coding-agent sessions across 1,639 repositories. Its data included sessions collected through April 2026 and covered tools such as Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, GitHub Copilot, and OpenCode.

Among the detected developer-agent misalignment episodes:

  • 38.33% involved violating a developer constraint.
  • 26.95% involved misreading the developer’s intent.
  • 22.58% involved inaccurate reporting about progress or completion.
  • 17.82% involved a faulty implementation.
  • 10.20% involved self-initiated action beyond the requested scope.

Where a visible resolution occurred in the conversation, 91.49% required explicit correction or pushback from the developer. In other words, the agent did not reliably correct its own direction. It first needed an attentive developer to notice the deviation. How Coding Agents Fail Their Users

This brings us back to the central problem: a developer who does not follow what happens behind the scenes may not even realize that the agent implemented a different task, violated a constraint, or declared success without satisfying the requirement.

Faster Code Can Create Faster Debt

Agent output is easy to count: files changed, commits created, pull requests opened, or lines written. The complexity and maintenance burden left behind are much harder to measure.

In a study published in March 2026, researchers analyzed 304,362 verified AI-authored commits across 6,275 GitHub repositories. Static analysis identified 484,606 issues introduced by those changes, with code smells accounting for 89.1% of them. More than 15% of the commits attributed to every studied AI assistant introduced at least one issue, and 24.2% of the tracked issues remained in the latest version of the repository. Debt Behind the AI Boom

Another longitudinal analysis published in January 2026 found that agent adoption produced substantial early velocity gains in some repositories, but little or only temporary additional throughput in projects that had previously used AI-enabled IDEs. In contrast, static-analysis warnings increased by roughly 18%, while cognitive complexity rose by about 35%. AI IDEs or Autonomous Agents?

These results do not mean that all agent-generated code is poor, just as human authorship does not guarantee good code. They warn against measuring success by generation speed alone. An agent can create technical debt at the same speed with which it creates features.

From Technical Debt to Knowledge Debt

Technical debt exists in the code: excessive coupling, duplication, fragile design, or a temporary workaround that quietly became permanent.

Agentic development can produce another kind of liability: knowledge debt—the gap between what the system can do and what its maintainers understand about it.

Knowledge debt accumulates when:

  • The agent changes components that nobody reads carefully.
  • Architectural decisions pass unnoticed inside a large diff.
  • The team relies on agent-generated tests to assess agent-generated code.
  • Everyone knows that the solution works, but nobody knows why.
  • Calling another agent becomes the only practical way to understand what the previous agent built.

The project may remain stable for a long time, so the problem does not appear urgent. It surfaces during an incident, a fundamental requirement change, the departure of the developer who supervised the agent, or the tool’s failure to recover the original context. The team then discovers that it owns the code but no longer owns enough knowledge to maintain it confidently.

The Productivity Gains Are Real—but They Are Not the Whole Story

A balanced discussion must acknowledge that agents also create real value.

In a METR survey conducted between February and April 2026, 349 technical workers estimated that AI had increased the value of their work by a median of approximately 1.4 to 2 times. Their median estimated speed increase was threefold. The researchers, however, cautioned that self-reported gains may overstate real-world improvement and that faster execution does not necessarily create an equivalent increase in value. METR’s 2026 technical-worker productivity survey

A study presented at MSR 2026 also found that human intervention occurred less frequently in agent-authored pull requests than in human-authored ones. When intervention was required, however, it involved longer durations and larger code changes. Guidance-level intervention accounted for 58.02% of the observed human effort, including constraining the agent’s actions and enforcing project conventions. The developer’s work was not eliminated; it shifted from direct implementation toward supervision, guidance, and quality control. Behind Agentic Pull Requests at MSR 2026

The right question is therefore not whether agents save time. In many tasks, they clearly do. The better question is:

What kind of time did we save, and what responsibility or knowledge did we give up in exchange?

How Can Developers Use Agents Without Becoming Spectators?

The solution is not to return to writing everything manually. Ignoring a tool that can automate repetitive work, explore a large repository, generate initial tests, or create a prototype quickly would make little sense. The goal is to use the agent without surrendering the developer’s role as decision-maker and accountable owner.

1. Do Not Accept Code You Cannot Explain

You do not need to memorize every line. You should, however, be able to explain the feature’s flow, transaction boundaries, architectural decisions, failure points, and effect on the surrounding system.

2. Review Decisions Before Reviewing Details

Before inspecting a large diff, ask the agent to state its assumptions, alternatives, and reasons for modifying each area. Excellent syntax review cannot rescue a solution built in the wrong direction.

3. Keep Changes Small Enough to Understand

The larger the change, the easier it becomes to hide a bad decision among hundreds of acceptable lines. Give every stage a narrow objective, explicit boundaries, and verifiable acceptance criteria.

4. Do Not Let the Agent Be the Only Witness to Its Success

If the same agent writes the code, creates the tests, runs them, and summarizes the result, then one source controls both implementation and evaluation. Use independent tests, static analysis, human review, and direct execution of important scenarios.

5. Ask for Evidence, Not Reassurance

There is a difference between “the problem is fixed” and showing the test that failed before the change and passed afterward. Require command output, behavioral comparisons, or other evidence appropriate to the task.

6. Use AI to Learn, Not Only to Obtain Answers

This is especially important for junior developers. Ask why an approach was selected, compare it with alternatives, and predict the result before running the code. An agent can be a powerful tutor, but it will not teach much if every instruction is simply “implement this and tell me when you are done.”

7. Preserve Your Ability to Work Without It

You do not need to reject AI to prove your competence. But you should not become unable to debug, read code, or make architectural decisions without consulting it. Periodically perform diagnosis and review yourself, particularly in critical parts of the system.

8. Do Not Confuse Passing Tests With a Correct Product

Tests prove only what they were designed to verify. Every test can pass while the requirement was misunderstood, an important case was omitted, or the solution became unnecessarily difficult to maintain.

Accountability Does Not Transfer to the Agent

An agent can write the code, run the tests, prepare the pull request, and produce a polished report claiming that the task is complete. It will not carry responsibility for a flawed architectural decision. It will not explain a data-loss incident to the customer, justify a leaked secret to the security team, or maintain a system that nobody inside the organization understands.

Coding tools are improving so quickly that some current measurements may become outdated within months. One principle is unlikely to age as quickly:

The more execution power you give the tool, the more judgment and review discipline you need to retain.

An agent does not automatically make a good developer worse, and it does not transform a junior developer into an expert. It is a force multiplier: used deliberately, it amplifies expertise; used without oversight, it amplifies ignorance and neglect.

Use it to increase your speed, not to replace your understanding. Only a developer who knows what is happening behind the scenes can decide when to trust the agent, when to stop it, and when to discard its work and begin again.

Evidence Note

This article relies primarily on research published in 2026. Some studies include data collected during 2026, while others analyze repositories or review activity from 2025. Several recent coding-agent studies are still preprints and have not completed every stage of peer review. Their findings should therefore be treated as timely and significant evidence, not as final conclusions about every tool, developer, or engineering organization.
